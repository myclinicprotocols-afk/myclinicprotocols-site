Standing Orders vs. Treatment Protocols

A standing order generally documents defined clinical authorization. A treatment protocol or SOP explains how the clinic carries out a service. The documents may be connected, but one should not be assumed to replace the other.

Practical comparison•Approximately 8 minutes•Updated September 7, 2026
Quick answer

A standing order tells qualified team members what actions are authorized, by whom, for which patients, and under what limits—when state law permits that structure. A protocol or SOP describes the approved workflow for screening, preparation, treatment, documentation, aftercare, and escalation. Clinics may need both.

The core difference is authorization versus workflow

Terminology varies across states and organizations. In general, a standing order is tied to an authorized prescriber or supervising professional and defines the circumstances under which another qualified team member may perform a specified action. A protocol or standard operating procedure focuses on how the clinic consistently completes the service.

Standing order

Defines authorized actions, patient conditions, responsible professionals, limits, escalation points, approval, and renewal.

Treatment protocol

Organizes the clinical standard from eligibility review through treatment documentation, aftercare, follow-up, and emergency response.

SOP

Translates policy into repeatable operational steps, such as room setup, inventory control, cleaning, handoffs, and records management.

Patient-specific order

Applies to an individual after an authorized professional evaluates the patient and enters the applicable order.

Names alone do not determine legal effect.

The substance of the document, state law, provider licenses, prescriptive or delegation authority, supervision arrangements, and the service being performed all matter.

When might a clinic need both?

A clinic may use a protocol to define the complete service and a standing order to authorize a narrower action within that service. For example, the protocol may describe screening, documentation, aftercare, and escalation while a separate order addresses whether a qualified professional may administer a specified medication under defined conditions.

  • Medication administration is part of the service
  • More than one provider role participates
  • Delegation or supervision is involved
  • A patient-specific evaluation is required
  • Clear stop and escalation criteria are needed
  • The clinic operates in more than one state

Map every action to a provider role

A useful document set answers who evaluates, diagnoses, orders or prescribes, performs the treatment, provides supervision, responds to complications, and approves the governing documents. Use actual license types rather than generic labels such as “provider.”

Evaluation

Identify who determines clinical appropriateness and whether that assessment must occur in person or may occur through an allowed modality.

Ordering and prescribing

Identify the professional with the required authority and how the order is documented.

Performance

Define which trained, qualified roles may perform each action and under what supervision.

Escalation

Name the clinical contact, urgent pathway, documentation steps, and situations requiring emergency services or referral.

Common documentation mistakes

  • Copying another clinic’s standing order without matching state law, licenses, products, or workflow.
  • Combining unrelated treatments into language too broad to guide safe practice.
  • Leaving authorization, scope limits, or escalation responsibility unclear.
  • Using inconsistent terms across the standing order, protocol, consent, and treatment record.
  • Failing to update documents after a provider, product, law, or workflow changes.

Pre-approval review checklist

Before implementation, compare the document set with the clinic’s state medical and nursing board requirements, scope-of-practice rules, supervision or delegation arrangements, prescribing information, product or device instructions, emergency capabilities, insurance requirements, and actual staff competencies.

Final approval belongs with the clinic.

The clinic’s qualified medical director, supervising provider, legal counsel, and other appropriate professionals should determine what authorization is required and approve the final documents before use.

Frequently asked questions

Is a protocol the same as a standing order?

No. A protocol generally explains the approved process. A standing order generally documents defined authorization, where allowed. The exact meaning and requirements depend on the jurisdiction and context.

Does every med spa treatment require a standing order?

Not necessarily. The answer depends on the service, medications or devices involved, state law, provider roles, and how orders are entered. The clinic should obtain state-specific professional review.

Can one standing order cover multiple treatments?

Possibly, but breadth can reduce clarity. Each authorized action, condition, limitation, role, and escalation point must still be specific enough for safe use and legally appropriate.

Who should sign the documents?

The appropriate signer depends on the document and state. Standing orders typically require an authorized prescriber or supervising professional; protocols also need documented clinical ownership and approval.

Educational information only. This article is not medical or legal advice and does not determine whether a standing order is required or valid in any jurisdiction. Requirements vary by state, service, license, ownership, supervision, and patient circumstances.
