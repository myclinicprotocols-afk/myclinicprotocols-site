Med Spa Consent Forms: What to Include

A strong consent form supports a real conversation. It identifies the specific procedure, explains material benefits and risks in understandable language, documents alternatives and questions, and matches the clinic’s protocol and patient record.

Procedure-specific checklist•Approximately 9 minutes•Updated September 7, 2026
Quick answer

Each consent should identify the procedure, purpose, expected benefits, material risks, alternatives—including no treatment—limitations, aftercare responsibilities, photography or privacy choices where relevant, questions, voluntary authorization, signatures, date, and the treating professional. State and procedure requirements must be reviewed separately.

A signature is not the whole consent process

Informed consent is a communication process, not merely a completed form. The patient needs an opportunity to receive understandable information, ask questions, consider alternatives, and make a voluntary decision. The signed document records that process; it does not replace it.

General intake

Collects medical history, medications, allergies, prior procedures, goals, and information needed for evaluation.

Procedure consent

Addresses the particular treatment, material risks, alternatives, limitations, and patient authorization.

Financial policy

Explains fees, deposits, cancellations, refunds, and payment responsibilities without being presented as clinical consent.

Photography authorization

Separates clinical documentation from optional marketing use and clearly states permitted uses.

One universal consent is rarely enough.

Neuromodulators, fillers, lasers, microneedling, IV therapy, and weight-management services have different risks, alternatives, workflows, and follow-up needs.

Core elements to evaluate

  • Patient and clinic identification
  • Exact procedure or treatment
  • Purpose and anticipated benefits
  • Material and procedure-specific risks
  • Reasonable alternatives and no treatment
  • Expected course and limitations
  • Pre-care and aftercare responsibilities
  • When and how to seek help
  • Opportunity for questions
  • Voluntary authorization
  • Interpreter or representative information
  • Patient and clinician signatures and date

Make the form procedure-specific

The consent should use the clinic’s actual product, device, technique, provider roles, and follow-up process. If a treatment is off-label, compounded, investigational, or used outside an FDA-cleared device indication, qualified clinical and legal reviewers should determine what additional disclosure is appropriate.

Build consent into the clinical workflow

1

Evaluate before presenting consent

Use the appropriate licensed professional to assess candidacy and identify patient-specific risks or reasons to defer.

2

Use the current approved version

Control forms by title, version, effective date, and approving provider so outdated copies are removed.

3

Support understanding

Use plain language, accessible formatting, appropriate interpretation, and time for questions rather than rushing at checkout.

4

Document the conversation

Record relevant questions, individualized risks, changes in the plan, signatures, date, and the professional obtaining consent.

Common mistakes

Generic risks

A long universal list can obscure the information most important for the actual treatment.

Marketing language

Consent should not promise outcomes or minimize uncertainty and potential complications.

Bundled permissions

Clinical consent, privacy acknowledgments, financial terms, and marketing photography should be clearly distinguished.

Outdated forms

Forms must change when products, devices, warnings, techniques, laws, or clinic workflows change.

Final review before use

Compare every consent with the corresponding protocol, treatment record, aftercare, product labeling, device instructions, state requirements, and clinic escalation plan. The clinic’s qualified medical director or supervising provider should approve the clinical content, with legal review when appropriate.

Frequently asked questions

Can one consent form cover every med spa service?

A general acknowledgment may supplement the record, but clinical consent should address the material information for the particular treatment.

Does a signed form protect the clinic from every claim?

No. A signature does not replace appropriate evaluation, communication, competent treatment, accurate documentation, or legal compliance.

Should consent be renewed at every visit?

The clinic’s qualified reviewers should set a policy based on the service, treatment series, changed circumstances, updated risks, and state requirements. Consent should be revisited whenever material information changes.

Can consent be electronic?

Often yes, but the system should preserve the approved content, identity, signature, date, audit trail, accessibility, and retention required for the clinic’s circumstances.

Educational information only. This article is not medical or legal advice and does not establish the consent requirements for any state, patient, or procedure. HHS research-consent materials are cited for general consent principles, not as direct rules for routine med spa care.
