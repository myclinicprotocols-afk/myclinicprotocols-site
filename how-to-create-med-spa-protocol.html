How to Create a Med Spa Protocol

A useful protocol is more than a list of treatment steps. It connects the service, provider roles, patient screening, products, equipment, documentation, safety planning, and approval process into one clinic-specific standard.

Quick answer

Start by defining the treatment and who may perform it. Then organize screening, product or device information, workflow, documentation, emergency response, training, version control, and final approval. State requirements and clinic circumstances must be reviewed separately.

First, separate the documents

Clinics often use “protocol,” “SOP,” and “standing order” as if they mean the same thing. They may work together, but each document has a different job. The exact legal effect of a standing order, delegation document, or supervisory agreement depends on the applicable state and provider roles.

Treatment protocol or SOP

Describes the clinic’s approved standard for screening, preparation, treatment workflow, documentation, aftercare, and escalation.

Standing order

Documents defined authorization from an appropriately qualified prescriber or supervising provider, when permitted and properly structured.

Patient consent

Supports a documented discussion of the procedure, expected benefits, material risks, alternatives, questions, and patient authorization.

Treatment record

Captures what occurred during an individual encounter, including product or device details, treatment information, response, and follow-up.

A protocol is not a substitute for authorization.

A polished document does not expand a professional license, create prescriptive authority, or replace required supervision, delegation, training, legal review, or clinical judgment.

The nine-part protocol framework

The framework below is intentionally high level. Clinical decisions, dosing, device settings, treatment parameters, and emergency instructions must come from qualified clinical leadership, applicable law, current evidence, manufacturer information, and the clinic’s actual resources.

1

Define the purpose and scope

Name the treatment, intended setting, approved use within the clinic, and what the document does and does not cover. Avoid combining unrelated treatments simply to reduce the number of documents.

2

Identify provider roles and oversight

Document who evaluates the patient, who may order or prescribe, who may perform the service, and who provides final clinical approval. Verify these roles against the clinic’s state, licenses, and supervisory structure.

3

Structure patient screening

Organize the history, eligibility considerations, contraindication review, allergies, relevant medications, baseline documentation, and escalation triggers that the approving provider requires.

4

Specify products, medications, and devices

Use the clinic’s actual products, formulations, concentrations, equipment models, supplies, and storage processes. Reference current prescribing information and manufacturer instructions rather than copying assumptions from another clinic.

5

Map the clinic workflow

Cover the sequence from consultation and preparation through treatment, documentation, aftercare, follow-up, and referral. Include responsibility handoffs so the process is usable by the team.

6

Address infection prevention and workplace safety

Connect the treatment protocol to applicable cleaning, sharps, exposure-control, PPE, waste-handling, and incident-reporting policies. Do not try to replace the clinic’s complete OSHA program with one treatment document.

7

Prepare supporting patient and clinic forms

Coordinate the consent, intake or eligibility form, treatment record, pre- and post-care instructions, room checklist, and adverse-event documentation so the same terms and workflow appear throughout the package.

8

Build the emergency and escalation pathway

Define recognition, immediate team roles, emergency resources, communication, transfer or referral, documentation, reporting, and follow-up according to the clinic’s approved plan and capabilities.

9

Add approval and version control

Include the document owner, effective date, version, review date, approving provider, and a process for changes when products, devices, staffing, laws, or clinical guidance change.

What should be customized for the clinic?

A generic template becomes useful only after it reflects the clinic that will rely on it. At minimum, the customization process should collect:

  • Clinic name, locations, and branding
  • Primary and additional operating states
  • Treating provider roles and licenses
  • Medical director or supervising provider
  • Selected treatments and service boundaries
  • Products, medications, and formulations
  • Device brands, models, and approved settings
  • Clinic techniques and workflow preferences
  • Available emergency medications and equipment
  • Documentation, follow-up, and escalation process

Common mistakes to avoid

Copying another clinic

Its staffing, products, equipment, state requirements, and oversight structure may not match yours.

Treating a template as approval

The purchasing clinic’s qualified medical director or supervising provider must complete the appropriate clinical review and approval.

Leaving forms inconsistent

The protocol, consent, treatment record, and aftercare documents should use matching terminology and instructions.

Publishing without ownership

A protocol needs an identified owner, effective date, review schedule, and controlled revision process.

Overlooking staff readiness

Written documents do not replace competency validation, training, emergency drills, or access to required resources.

Making broad compliance claims

No template can guarantee compliance without reviewing the clinic’s complete facts and current federal, state, and local requirements.

Final review and implementation

Before use, the clinic should compare the draft with current prescribing information, manufacturer instructions, applicable laws and board guidance, facility policies, professional licensing requirements, and its actual clinical workflow. The approving provider should resolve unanswered clinical questions, document approval, communicate the final version, and ensure affected team members are trained.

Frequently asked questions

Who should approve a med spa treatment protocol?

The clinic should identify the appropriately qualified medical director, supervising provider, prescriber, or other authorized professional based on the treatment, state, ownership structure, and provider roles.

Is a purchased protocol automatically compliant?

No. A template or customized draft still requires review against the clinic’s current circumstances, applicable law, board requirements, manufacturer information, and professional judgment.

Can one protocol cover several treatments?

Closely related services may share certain policies, but each treatment should be clear enough that the team can identify its specific screening, products, workflow, documentation, and emergency considerations.

How often should protocols be reviewed?

Use a defined periodic review schedule and conduct an earlier review whenever relevant laws, guidance, products, equipment, staffing, clinical leadership, or workflows change.
