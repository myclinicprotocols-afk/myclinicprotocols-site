IV Hydration Protocol & Standing Orders

An IV program needs a documented line from patient evaluation and prescribing authority to the exact formulation, preparation, administration, monitoring, discharge, and emergency response.

Clinic workflow checklist•Approximately 10 minutes•Updated September 7, 2026
Quick answer

An IV hydration package should define who evaluates and prescribes, the authorized formulations and patient criteria, ingredient sourcing and preparation, vascular access and administration roles, baseline and ongoing monitoring, stop criteria, discharge, emergency response, adverse-event reporting, training, and document approval.

Standing orders and protocols do different jobs

The standing order or patient-specific order documents appropriate authorization. The protocol explains how the approved service is performed. Whether standing orders are permitted—and what they must contain—depends on state law, provider roles, prescriptive authority, ownership, and the clinical model.

A menu is not a medical order.

Marketing names such as “energy” or “immunity” do not identify ingredients, doses, compatibility, indications, contraindications, or the authorized prescriber’s decision.

The complete document set

Clinical protocol

Scope, roles, evaluation, access, preparation, administration, monitoring, discharge, and escalation.

Orders

Authorized formulations or patient-specific orders with criteria, exclusions, limits, and prescriber approval.

Patient forms

Medical intake, consent, medication education, administration record, aftercare, and urgent contact instructions.

Operational safety

Inventory, storage, temperature, preparation area, infection control, exposure response, emergency checks, incidents, and training.

Seven workflow questions the protocol must answer

1

Who evaluates and prescribes?

Define the required assessment, authorized professional, order-entry process, and when consultation or referral is required.

2

What exactly is administered?

Document each ingredient, concentration, amount, diluent, final volume, source, lot, expiration, compatibility review, and approved formulation.

3

Who prepares and administers?

Match each action to a qualified role, training, supervision, clean preparation process, independent checks, labeling, and traceability.

4

How is the patient monitored?

Define baseline information, reassessment, infusion checks, response documentation, stop criteria, and access to the responsible clinician.

5

How are complications handled?

Address infiltration or extravasation, allergic reaction, syncope, medication error, infection concern, clinical deterioration, EMS activation, transfer, and follow-up.

Administration-record checklist

  • Patient, date, indication, and assessment
  • Prescriber and exact order
  • Ingredients, concentrations, and amounts
  • Source, lot, expiration, and preparation
  • Access site and responsible professional
  • Start, monitoring, completion, and response
  • Changes, interruption, or discontinuation
  • Education and discharge condition
  • Adverse event and escalation
  • Follow-up plan and communications

Safe injection and emergency readiness

CDC recommends aseptic technique, a new sterile needle and syringe for every injection, single-patient use of infusion and administration sets, and single-dose vials whenever possible. The clinic’s policy should also address hand hygiene, medication preparation areas, labeling, sharps, exposure control, cleaning, storage, and waste.

Emergency medications, equipment, staff roles, clinical contact, EMS activation, transfer agreements or pathways, documentation, and drills should reflect the clinic’s formulations, patient population, staffing, and location.

Frequently asked questions

Can a standing order cover multiple IV formulations?

Possibly, where allowed, but each formulation, ingredient, patient criterion, exclusion, limit, role, and escalation point must remain clear and appropriately authorized.

Can non-prescribers select ingredients?

Provider authority depends on state law and licensure. The protocol should not allow a menu or staff preference to replace an authorized prescriber’s decision.

Does the protocol need exact doses?

The clinic’s authorized prescriber must approve exact formulation information using current clinical, pharmacy, legal, and patient-specific considerations.
